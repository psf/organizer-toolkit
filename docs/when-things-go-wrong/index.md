# When Things Go Wrong

Even if something feels like a "failure" in the moment, just know that it's okay and that this happens to *everyone*. No one is blaming you for things that go unexpectedly.

Also keep in mind that you as the organizer will notice things that go "wrong" but most of them no one else will notice at all.

## "Hello, Murphy. Welcome."

Whatever can go wrong eventually will, usually at the least convenient moment. Accept that Murphy works in mysterious, unpredictable ways. A sense of humor, a light grip, and resourcefulness are what carry an organizer through.

People are human, too. We all get sick, overwhelmed, and forgetful, and we all make mistakes. Try to hold these as the "oh, goodness" moments of volunteering rather than as failures.

## Reducing the Damage Before It Happens

- Arrive early enough to test the projector, the sound, and the internet before anyone else does.

- Have a plan for the moment nothing works. Someone can always give a talk from a laptop screen if the projector dies.

- Put one person on the door specifically to greet people, so no one walks in feeling invisible while you are busy firefighting.

- Say the Code of Conduct out loud at the start, and know in advance who handles a report and how. You do not want to be improvising in a charged moment. See [Governance, Trademarks, and Policies](../governance/index.md).

- Leave time in the schedule that is not programmed. Slack in the agenda absorbs delays.

- Make sure no single task depends entirely on one person showing up. An unowned or single-owner area is where things quietly fail.

## When the Turnout Is Smaller Than You Hoped

Your first event, or your first event back after a break, may be smaller than you expected. That is normal and it is not failure. Communities compound. The handful of people in a borrowed room are often the same people who years later chair conferences and lead teams.

If only one speaker turns up, that is enough. If none do, an open Q&A or a group discussion still makes the evening worthwhile, and getting everyone together matters more than filling the full hour.

## Afterward

- Write down what went wrong while it is still fresh. Future you will thank present you.

- Fix the process, not the person. Most "failures" are missing checklists, not bad volunteers.

- Share what you learned with your co-organizers, and with other groups if it would help them. Nearly every problem you hit has been hit before by someone happy to talk about it.

- Be gentle with yourself. Repeated stress from things going wrong is a fast route to [burnout](../burnout/index.md).
