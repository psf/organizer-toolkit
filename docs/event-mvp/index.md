# Event MVP

## Minimum "requirements" for organizing an event

**Good news! There's no hard requirements or one "correct" way to organize an event.** But planning ahead can improve your event and your experience as an organizer.

**In-person events help build community.** There are many types of events you can organize, and they don't need to be complicated or "perfect" to be effective.

You don't need a lot of people for an event to be a success, usually communities and events start little and they grow organically.

Some examples of events are:

- **Community Meetups**: Casual gatherings for networking, sharing updates, and brainstorming.
- **Talk Nights / Lightning Talks**: Community members share knowledge in short talks (5–20 min).
- **Coffee Chats / Open Hours**: Regular informal virtual/in-person hangouts.
- **Workshops**: Hands-on sessions (e.g., intro to Git, testing frameworks, contributing to OSS).
- **Study Groups**: Regular meetups to learn a tool, framework, or language together.
- **Coworking sessions:** Meet in a local coffee store or somewhere similar to work.
- **Sprints / Hack sessions**: Hands-on coding time, often on an open source project.
- **Code reviews**: Volunteers bring code and the group works through it together.
- **Video & discussion nights**: Watch a short conference talk (10–15 min), then discuss it as a group. Far more fun than watching alone at your desk.
- **Show-and-tell**: No fixed topic; people talk about what they're working on or what they've found interesting lately.

### Deciding the format and who leads it

One of the first decisions is what kind of event this actually is, because that shapes everything else: the venue, the length, how you promote it, and who you need on the team. Python events are most often talks, sometimes workshops, and occasionally sprints or hackathons. The choice belongs to you and your local group.

It helps for each event to have someone acting as the **technical lead**, a person familiar enough with the topic to plan the content, find presenters, and either answer questions or know where to find answers. They don't have to be the one presenting every time. This role is as much about people as technology: making attendees feel comfortable asking questions, and making them want to come back.

**No expert available?** Run it as a *study group* instead. If someone is interested in learning a topic but nobody is an expert yet, make it explicit that everyone is at the same level and the group will figure it out together. You don't have to literally call it a study group, but the host should set that tone early. This takes enormous pressure off you and is often a better learning experience anyway.

Organizing an event isn't necessarily complicated, but it can take time, so having a checklist is helpful, especially to divide tasks.

## Example Checklist for an In-Person Meetup with Talks

- Set the date and time (Find the day of the week that works best for your community).
- Find a venue: local tech companies or universities can sometimes host the event.
- Call for talks: ask in tech groups, via DM, etc.
- Be persistent: follow up and reconfirm talks, workshops, or other activities.
- Create the event on [meetup.com](https://meetup.com); you can duplicate a previous event.
- Make a flyer (you can reuse designs from past events or use [canva.com](https://canva.com)):
    - Logo
    - Date
    - Time
    - Venue
    - Talks, workshops, activities
    - Meetup event link
- Post on social media (include both the flyer and event details).
- Share the event in other IT groups or communities.
- Open registration (RSVP) and set a cap if your venue has one.
- Send reminders of the event in your group every few days, especially the day before and the day of the event.
- Confirm the venue, and arrive early on the day to test the projector, sound, and internet.

For different types of events, the checklist will probably be different. A good idea is to **review the checklist after your event**, and if there were any lessons learned or things to improve for next time, update it accordingly.

## Finding Speakers and Setting the Agenda

Events with talks live and die on the quality of the talks. Too few and the event may not feel worth attending; too many and it drags and people lose interest. Aim for variety rather than three talks that cover the same ground, and talk to your speakers in advance so nothing lands off-topic.

### Getting speakers

Asking in person at your events is the single most effective method: people who have already shown up are far likelier to say yes. Beyond that, write directly to people whose work you admire, or put out an open call. A sample call you can adapt:

> Hello fellow Pythonistas!
>
> Thanks for being part of the group. We're looking for speakers for our upcoming meetups. If you have anything to share about Python (a framework, Python in education, or how Python is used in your industry), we'd love to hear from you. Even loosely related topics are welcome.
>
> Some ideas we're interested in:
>
> - A deep dive into a specific Python feature
> - A novel use of Python that made your life easier
> - A project you built with Python
> - How you used Python to solve a problem at work, school, or anywhere else
> - An interesting side project you've been playing with
>
> New and experienced speakers are equally welcome. Talks can be up to about 30 minutes, or as short as you'd like.

Announce your call for speakers at least three weeks before the event. That gives people time to say yes *and* prepare.

### Structuring the agenda

- Two or three talks of roughly 25–30 minutes each, with Q&A after each one, is a reliable shape. One longer talk is fine if the topic really matters to your community.
- **Schedule the first talk to start at least 20 minutes after doors open; 30 minutes is better.** This gives people time to arrive, grab a snack, and lets latecomers in without interrupting a speaker.
- If you can only find one speaker, that's fine. Fill the remaining time with open Q&A, group discussion, or lightning talks.
- If you have *no* speakers, it is still worth meeting. Getting everyone in a room together has value on its own.

### Other fun ways to set an agenda

- **[Open space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_Space_Technology)**: no pre-set agenda. Participants propose topics on the spot and cluster with others interested in the same thing.
- **Lightning talks**: 2–5 minute slots on anything. Preparing a full talk is intimidating; almost anyone can talk for three minutes about something they love.
- **[PechaKucha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PechaKucha) / [Ignite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ignite_%28event%29) style**: 20 slides, ~20 seconds each. Fast, funny, and a great equalizer.
- **Video and discussion**: open with a short talk recording, then spend the rest of the time on group discussion. Attendees can share related articles, their own experience, or questions the video raised.

## Venues and Online Events

As a general rule, you can very often get space for free or at minimal cost. Good targets are coworking spaces, hackerspaces, universities, local companies, and startups. Many are happy to lend a room, especially outside working hours or at weekends.

Another trick: look at where other meetups in your city host theirs, and ask those organizers how they arranged it. They've usually already done the research and will happily share what they learned.

For **online events**, pick a platform your community can reliably access and that lets you record sessions if you want to. Some communities have licenses through a sponsor or partner organization worth asking about. Whatever you choose, test it before the day.

## Promotion, RSVPs, and Reminders

An event attended only by its organizers is easier to run but far less rewarding. Promote where your people already are: local tech groups, university mailing lists, WhatsApp or Telegram groups, LinkedIn, X, and word of mouth. **Personal invitations convert much better than public posts**: asking ten people directly will fill a room faster than broadcasting to a thousand.

- Aim to start publicizing **at least 3–4 weeks before** the event. Earlier is better; it gives people time to plan and builds anticipation.
- Don't only target computer science departments. Students and lecturers across many fields are enthusiastic about learning something new, so reach beyond the obvious.
- A short email that a university, faculty union, or student body can forward on your behalf goes a long way.
- Event platforms like [Meetup](https://meetup.com), Eventbrite, and Luma will also surface your event to people who aren't following you yet.

### Handling RSVPs

Ask people to RSVP even when the event is free. It won't be exact (some sign up and don't come, others turn up without signing up), but it tells you roughly how much food to get and whether you'll exceed the room's capacity.

- Open RSVPs well ahead of the event, set a maximum cap, and put extra people on a waiting list.
- Send a reminder about a week out, and another two days before with location details and directions. Ask anyone who can no longer attend to cancel so you can offer their place to the waiting list.
- **Reminders meaningfully increase turnout.** Without them, people who RSVP'd weeks ago simply forget.
- Also collect sign-in details from people who *actually* attend: name, email, which event, and whether they're new or returning. This is how you learn whether people are coming back, and how you invite them to the next one.

## Sharing the Load: Organizer Roles

Organizing with a team isn't mandatory, but it's strongly advisable, mostly because it's how you avoid burning out. If you do work as a team, agree a few ground rules up front about who decides what, to avoid misunderstandings later.

A simple split that works well:

- **Media / comms**: event page, call for speakers, opening and managing RSVPs, collecting slides, posting during the event, writing the recap afterward.
- **Setup / logistics**: venue confirmation, refreshments, sign-in sheet, arriving early, checking equipment, photos and video.
- **Host / MC**: welcomes people, shares the agenda and housekeeping, introduces speakers, points people to the feedback form, keeps things moving.

For a small event one person can wear two hats. What matters is that none of these areas is left unowned. Unowned areas are where things quietly go wrong.

## Running the Event on the Day

Aim to be at the venue **at least 45 minutes early**. Walk the space, test the equipment, coordinate with whoever is hosting you, and be visible for early arrivals who aren't sure they're in the right place.

A simple run of show for the host:

1. Introduce yourself and welcome everyone, especially anyone who looks unsure.
2. Cover the practical stuff: fire exits, toilets, wifi password.
3. Go through the Code of Conduct. Saying it out loud sets a clear expectation and signals what kind of room this is. See [Governance, Trademarks, and Policies](../governance/index.md).
4. Outline the agenda so people know what's happening and when.
5. An icebreaker or round of quick introductions, if it suits your group. You can also save introductions for later so latecomers aren't left out.
6. Introduce the speaker and topic, then get on with it.
7. Recognize and thank newcomers by name if they're comfortable with it.
8. Thank the speakers and the venue host at the end.

Take photos and video during the event, and post a few on social media as it happens; it draws in people who didn't know it was on.

## After the Event

The hour or two you spend closing the loop afterward is what turns a one-off event into a community.

- **Collect materials the same day.** Ask speakers for slides, repos, and links while it's fresh. Chase it a day later and it takes three weeks.
- **Publish a short recap** within a few days: a blog post or social thread with photos, links to slides, and any recordings. Share it with attendees as well as publicly.
- **Thank the venue host and speakers** by email within a couple of days, and give them public credit by name.
- **Pass feedback back to speakers** individually, including the good parts. It's what makes them want to speak again.

### Collecting feedback

Feedback can be an open discussion at the end or a simple form. If you use a form, share the link before people leave and give them about ten minutes to fill it in. Response rates collapse once everyone's gone home.

Don't expect everyone to reply. Anywhere from 15% to 70% is normal, and a high response rate is itself usually a sign the event landed well.

A short survey is plenty:

- This event was worth my time. *(Agree 5 / 4 / 3 / 2 / 1 Disagree)*
- I would recommend future events to a friend or colleague. *(Agree 5 / 4 / 3 / 2 / 1 Disagree)*
- What I liked best was...
- Something that could be improved is...
- Any other comments?

Adapt these freely; this is a starting point, not a standard. The point is to learn something you can act on, not to collect data for its own sake.

It's also worth tracking a few aggregate numbers per event: total attendees, how many were newcomers, and how many were returning. You never need to share anyone's contact details to do this, and those three numbers tell you more about your community's health than almost anything else. See [Building and Growing a Community](../growing-a-community/index.md).

## Your First Event

Everything above is a guide, not a rulebook. For a genuine first event, keep it small and warm:

1. **Welcome everyone properly.** People love feeling at home somewhere that isn't home. A warm opening makes it much easier for them to talk to strangers.
2. **Run through the Code of Conduct.**
3. **Do introductions.** Name, what they do, what they're hoping to get out of the group. Keep it brief.
4. **Say something about Python and the community**, locally and globally: what it is, what it's used for, and why it's worth learning. If you're not sure about the wider community, ask another organizer to fill you in.
5. **Find out who's in the room.** Who's new to programming entirely? New to Python specifically? Already experienced? This tells you what to plan next, and experienced attendees are often glad to talk about projects they've worked on and problems they hit.
6. **Introduce your speaker**, if you have one. If you found more than one for a first event, that's a genuinely great start.
7. **Set a rhythm and say it out loud.** Monthly, quarterly, whatever you can sustain. You don't need exact dates yet, but tell people how often you intend to meet so they don't assume the group died in the gap.
8. **Ask for volunteers.** Invite anyone interested to take on a role for next time. This is how you get co-organizers.

Your first event may be smaller than you hoped. That's normal, and it isn't failure. See [When Things Go Wrong](../when-things-go-wrong/index.md).

## Additional Tips for Organizing Events

- **Start simple** and keep costs, both time and money, low. For example, coffee and food can enhance the experience, but they're not essential. Don't include them in your events unless the costs are easily covered, such as through a sponsor.
- **Celebrate small wins.** Thank volunteers and speakers, and highlight what went well. It encourages more people to get involved next time.
- Keep a list of venues where you've hosted events, along with the contact person. This can be useful for planning future events or if someone else needs to organize the next one.
- **Take pictures!** Keeping memories is great and you can use the pictures to promote future events. Just make sure you have permission from the people in the photos before sharing them publicly.
- **Invite attendees to give a talk** at the next event or to help organize it.
- **Ask for feedback** on what you could improve for the next event.
- **Start and end with community.** Give time before/after the event for people to talk. Community often grows in the gaps between the scheduled activities.
- **Document everything.** Create shared folders or documents for checklists, templates, budgets, flyers, etc. It'll save you and others tons of time when organizing future events.
- **Protect a low-effort default.** Keep one format you can run on a tired week with almost no preparation, such as a coworking session or lightning talk night. It can save a month you'd otherwise skip entirely.
