## Email

We recommend that all Python software communities have their own general email address (e.g. pythonghana.org). You and your other co-organizers can manage that email address how you'd like (e.g. all co-organizers can log in; all email is forwarded; only one organizer manages it; etc).

It is also good practice to keep personal and community communications separate. You can easily do this if you use the Chrome browser and set up [two separate profiles](https://support.google.com/chrome/answer/2364824?hl=en)– one for personal and one for your community.

## Account & Password Management

Please sign up for all accounts related to your Python software community with your designated email address. This is so that handover to a future organizer is a lot smoother, and account recovery is easier. We highly suggest you use a unique password for each account you use. An example is [LastPass](https://lastpass.com/), a password manager (so you don't have to remember every unique password) that allows you to create unique and difficult-to-guess passwords, as well as save them for you.

[LastPass](https://lastpass.com/) also allows you to [share](https://helpdesk.lastpass.com/sharing/) passwords with folks; so you can set up your local Python community Facebook account, then store that password in [LastPass](https://lastpass.com/), and allow your co-organizers to log in with that stored password by sharing it.

### How do I ask the PSF to cover Meetup fees?

The PSF financially hosts worldwide Python Meetups on their [Meetup Pro Network](https://www.meetup.com/pro/python-software-foundation-meetups) as long as 3 conditions are met:

- The Meetup holds at least 2 events per calendar year (virtual or in-person).
- Event content focuses on Python content at least 70% of the time. Content may include adjacent topics, such as networking, basic machine learning, etc.
- The Meetup posts your community Code of Conduct in the About tab of the Meetup page to become and stay eligible for the PSF Meetup Pro network.

Please [submit a grant request from the PSF](https://psfmember.org/grant-proposals/). In order to access the form, you may first have to create a free account on the [psfmember.org](https://psfmember.org/) website. Please send questions to grants@pyfound.org.

## Legal Status, Registration, and Fiscal Sponsorship

!!! warning "This is not legal or tax advice"

    Company, charity, and tax law differ enormously between countries, and sometimes between regions of the same country. Everything below is intended to help you ask the right questions of a local lawyer or accountant, not to replace one.

Sooner or later, usually the first time a sponsor asks "who exactly are we paying?", organizers run into a question this toolkit cannot answer for you: does our community need to be a legally registered organization?

This section explains how the relationship with the PSF actually works, how to recognize when you have outgrown an informal arrangement, and what your options are.

### The PSF does not legally represent your community

This is the single most important thing to understand, and it surprises a lot of organizers.

The Python Software Foundation supports the global community in many ways: it authorizes the use of its trademarks such as "PyCon" and "PyLadies", it awards grants, it covers Meetup Pro fees, and it maintains the infrastructure the whole ecosystem depends on. **What it does not do is own, operate, or act as the legal entity for local Python groups.**

Your community is legally independent. It exists under the laws of your own country, and it is your organizing team, not the PSF, that is responsible for its contracts, its money, and its obligations.

This is deliberate, and it cuts both ways:

- **It protects the PSF.** A single US-based charity cannot take on the legal liability and regulatory reporting of hundreds of autonomous groups operating under dozens of legal systems.
- **It protects your autonomy.** You are not a branch office. You set your own direction, choose your own venues and sponsors, and run your community in a way that fits your local culture.
- **It puts the responsibility on you.** Being authorized to use the name "PyCon" does not make the PSF a party to your venue contract or your sponsorship agreements. Nobody at the PSF can sign on your behalf.

So when a company asks who they are transferring money to, or a venue asks which organization is on the contract, "the Python Software Foundation" is not the answer. The answer has to be either a legal entity of your own, a partner entity acting on your behalf, or an individual organizer, and that last option is the one that causes problems.

### Signs you may need to register

Most groups start informally, and that is completely fine. A meetup that borrows a room and spends nothing does not need paperwork. Watch for these signals that you are approaching the limit:

- A sponsor cannot pay you because their finance process requires an invoice from a registered entity with a tax identification number.
- You are asked to go through corporate vendor or procurement onboarding, which usually requires registration documents and bank details in the organization's name.
- You need a bank account in the community's name because money is currently sitting in an organizer's personal account.
- A venue wants a contract signed by an organization, or requires proof of public liability insurance.
- You are selling tickets, which in many jurisdictions creates tax and consumer-protection obligations.
- A grant, university partnership, or government programme requires a registered counterparty.
- The amounts involved are large enough that you are uncomfortable with one person being personally liable, or personally taxed, on money that belongs to the community.

That last point deserves emphasis. **Running community money through an organizer's personal bank account is the most common and most damaging shortcut in community organizing.** It can create a genuine personal tax liability, it makes clean [handover](../handing-over-a-community/index.md) nearly impossible, and it exposes an individual to risk for decisions the group made collectively.

### What registering usually looks like

The available structures and the names for them vary widely by country. Common forms include:

- A **registered society, association, or club**: often the lightest-weight option, and in many countries designed precisely for volunteer groups.
- A **non-profit company**: for example, a company limited by guarantee, or an equivalent non-share-capital company.
- An **NGO, trust, or foundation**, depending on local law.
- An **unincorporated association**, which in some jurisdictions is enough to open a bank account and receive funds without full incorporation.

Registration is not free and it is not a one-time task. Expect some combination of registration fees, a constitution or governing document, named officers or directors, annual filings, and possibly an audit. Some countries also offer charitable or tax-exempt status separately from registration, which can matter a great deal to local sponsors who want a tax receipt.

Register when the benefits clearly outweigh that ongoing administrative load, and not before.

### Alternatives to registering right now

Registration is not the only way to accept support, and often not the fastest.

- **Ask a sponsor to pay suppliers directly.** Let them settle the venue invoice or the catering bill themselves. The money never touches your hands, and most sponsors are perfectly happy with this.
- **Take in-kind sponsorship instead of cash.** Venue space, food, printing, swag, and cloud credits are all real support that sidesteps the problem entirely.
- **Partner with an entity that already exists.** A university department, a local tech non-profit, a hackerspace, or a friendly company can sometimes receive and hold funds on your behalf. Put the arrangement in writing, however friendly it is, so everyone understands who controls the money and what happens if the relationship ends.
- **Work with a local fiscal host.** Some countries have organizations whose specific purpose is holding funds for unincorporated community projects. This is the same idea as fiscal sponsorship, applied locally.
- **Apply for a PSF grant.** The [Grants Program](https://www.python.org/psf/grants/) is genuinely global: in 2023 the PSF distributed grants to 174 groups across 52 countries. Read the current requirements on the application form, and ask grants@pyfound.org if you are unsure whether your group's structure qualifies.

### What fiscal sponsorship is

**Fiscal sponsorship** is an arrangement where an established non-profit extends its own legal and tax-exempt status to a project that is not a separate legal entity. The sponsored project gets to operate under the sponsor's umbrella: it can receive donations, hold funds, and sign agreements through the sponsor, without incorporating itself.

In practice, that typically means the sponsoring organization handles:

- Receiving donations and sponsorship, often with tax-deductible receipts for donors.
- Accounting, budgeting, and cash flow.
- Insurance and contract or legal support.
- Access to non-profit discounts and pricing.

In exchange, the project accepts the sponsor's oversight: its activities have to fit the sponsor's charitable mission, and the sponsor has final say over how the funds are used.

### The PSF's fiscal sponsorship programme

The PSF does run a fiscal sponsorship programme. It provides sponsorees with 501(c)(3) tax-exempt status and manages back-office issues including cash flow, accounting, budgeting, insurance, and contract and legal support, plus access to non-profit discounts. Current sponsorees include local user groups such as Boston Python, ChiPy, PyRVA, and the Denver Python Users Group; regional conferences such as PyCascades, PyOhio, and North Bay Python; and open source projects such as Pallets, Twisted, and Jazzband.

Two important caveats:

1. **The PSF is not accepting new fiscal sponsoree requests at this time.** Check the [Fiscal Sponsorees page](https://www.python.org/psf/fiscal-sponsorees/) for the current status before planning around it. When applications are open, the process starts with an email to fiscal-sponsors@python.org, followed by a questionnaire and an introductory call about how your event, group, or project fits the PSF's charitable mission. Reviews are generally paused in the month before and after PyCon US.

2. **In practice the programme has been limited to groups and projects based in the United States.** Every geographically-based fiscal sponsoree is a US community or conference. This is a consequence of the legal mechanism rather than a lack of interest: 501(c)(3) is a US tax status, and extending a US charity's umbrella over an organization operating under another country's law is legally and administratively complex.

**If your community is outside the US, plan on the assumption that you will need a local solution**: either your own registration, a local partner entity, or a local fiscal host. Do not build a conference budget around receiving PSF fiscal sponsorship.

Note the distinction clearly, because it is easy to conflate the two: the PSF's **grants** are worldwide, while its **fiscal sponsorship** is US-centric and currently closed to new applicants.

### Practical next steps

1. **Ask other Python communities in your country or region how they did it.** This is by far the fastest route to a good answer, because they have already navigated the same legal system. The [conferences mailing list](https://mail.python.org/mailman/listinfo/conferences) and neighbouring user groups are good places to ask.
2. **Talk to a local lawyer or accountant** before registering anything. Many will advise a volunteer community at low cost or for free, and a single conversation can save you from choosing the wrong structure.
3. **Separate community money from personal money as early as you can**, even if that means the simplest possible arrangement in the short term.
4. **Write down whatever you decide** and keep it in your handover documentation, including who the officers are, where the registration documents live, and what the annual filing obligations are.
